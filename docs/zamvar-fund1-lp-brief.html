A machine that trades the week around biotech catalysts — with a memory that compounds.

Every week, a team of AI analysts studies the biotech companies about to hit a make-or-break moment — an FDA decision, a trial result — and builds a small, hedged, one-week portfolio. Every trade is checked by an independent second opinion and approved by a human before any money moves. This document explains exactly how the system works, why we believe it has an edge, and precisely how we intend to prove it.

01

Why the edge exists

An opportunity is only real if there's a reason others leave it on the table. Here's the reason.

When a biotech company approaches a scheduled catalyst — a regulator's approval decision, a clinical trial's headline result — its stock is priced on a guess about a binary outcome. When the answer arrives, the price re-rates hard. These are among the most reliably large moves in public markets. But three features make them genuinely hard to trade, and that difficulty is the opportunity:

Fat-tailed

The move is rarely small

Across the catalysts we studied, the typical event moved the stock more than any normal week — and roughly one in five moved it 70%+ in a single window. The middle is a coin flip; the tails are the whole game.

Skewed down

More often bad than good

Slightly more than half of these events resolved against the stock. Simply "buying the exciting story" is a losing strategy before costs — direction has to be earned, not assumed.

Expectation-driven

Good news can still fall

A drug can succeed and the stock still drops, because the success was already priced in. Reading what the market already expects is as important as reading the science.

Structural gap

Who's on the other side

Retail chases the narrative; generalist funds lack the reference library to judge each event; specialists rarely apply systematic, repeatable discipline. We aim to sit precisely in that gap.

The edge is never "which stock." It's "which direction, how big, and is it already in the price" — answered the same disciplined way, every week, with a memory that gets better.

What we trade — and why these names

The universe isn't a hype list. It's an empirically-chosen set of names that actually move on their events and can be traded cleanly.

We built the universe backwards from evidence. We looked at five years of catalysts for public biotech companies and measured, name by name, how much each stock actually moved on its own catalysts — how often it made a big move, and how big. We kept the names with a proven history of tradeable movement, then filtered for the practical necessities: enough liquidity to get in and out at fair prices, and listed options so the market's own expectations are visible. The result is roughly 73 companies spanning the breadth of therapeutic areas.

The two disciplines that matter most:

Provenance

Every fact carries its source

Nothing enters the system without a citation to where it came from. A claim the desk can't source is dropped, not guessed. That makes every decision auditable back to a primary document.

No look-ahead

The system can't cheat with hindsight

Every fact is time-stamped with when it became knowable, so when we test the strategy on history it can only ever use what was truly available at the time. This is the single most important safeguard for honest testing — and the easiest one for a manager to get quietly wrong.

04

The desk — how a decision is made

Four specialist roles plus a human gate. No single voice can put on a trade.

Each week the desk works like a disciplined investment committee, staffed by AI analysts with defined roles. Crucially, the analyst that wants to make a trade is not the one that approves it — an independent reviewer forms its own opinion, and a human signs off before anything trades.

The decision pipeline — every trade passes through all of it
Catalyst Desk
Reads the event and the company and calls the direction — is this more likely to go up or down, and why.
Trade Builder
Estimates how big the move should be — using the "football field" (Section 05): several independent methods, not one guess.
Due Diligence
An independent second desk that re-derives its own number from scratch — a genuine check, not a rubber stamp.
Managing Director
Compares the two. If the Builder and DD disagree beyond tolerance, it's sent back to be re-worked before it can proceed.
Human Gate
A person approves every trade after reviewing the dossier. No live trade happens without it — a hard rule, not a setting.
Execution
Approved trades route through the brokerage's sanctioned agent-trading system, with a per-trade notification to the human.

05

How we generate edge

This is the heart of it — three ideas working together.

1 · A memory that compounds

The desk reasons the way a seasoned analyst does — from precedent. We built a playbook of hundreds of past catalysts, each stored not just as a number but as a short case study: what the setup was, what happened, and why the stock moved the way it did. When a new event arrives, the desk pulls up the past situations that genuinely rhyme with it — same kind of disease, same kind of drug, same kind of setup (for example, "a well-liked result that was already priced in") — and reasons from what actually happened to those. Every week adds new cases, so the memory sharpens over time. This is the asset that grows.

2 · The football field — magnitude from convergence

To size the expected move, we never trust a single model. We compute it several independent ways and look for where they agree — the way an appraiser triangulates a home's value from comparable sales, replacement cost, and income. When the methods converge, conviction is high; when they scatter, the system flags low confidence and steps back.

3 · Ruthless downside discipline

The tails cut both ways, so survival comes first. Positions are sized so that no single event can seriously hurt the book; the desk actively avoids "falling knives" (broken stories that keep falling); and the long-only mandate is hedged with paired positions rather than naked bets. The goal is to be around for the winners — you cannot compound if you blow up.

To test whether the strategy works, we use the disciplines a professional allocator expects:

Walk-forward

Test only on the untouched future

We build on the past, then test on a later period the system never saw, and roll forward. Combined with the no-look-ahead time-stamping, this prevents the classic self-deception of a strategy that only "works" in hindsight.

Real costs

Priced as if traded

Every simulated trade pays realistic transaction costs (the spread you actually cross). A strategy that only works before costs isn't a strategy.

Benchmarked

Measured against biotech itself

Returns are judged against the biotech index — beating a rising tide, not riding it.

Deflated

Penalized for luck

We use a performance measure that explicitly discounts for the many strategy variations tried — so we don't mistake a lucky configuration for genuine skill.

What our first backtest showed — and what it did NOT

A 52-week walk-forward turned a simulated $10,000 into ~$11,800, with positions correctly entering and exiting on the weekly cadence, net of costs. We treat this as system-validation — proof that the machinery works end-to-end — and explicitly NOT as a return forecast or evidence of skill. On the luck-adjusted measure, the result is not yet distinguishable from chance. That is the honest read, and it's exactly why the next step is a live proving period, not a capital raise on a backtest.

07

Risk management

Binary events demand that risk control be structural, not discretionary.

A single event goes catastrophically wrong
Survival-first sizing. No position is large enough that one bad binary meaningfully impairs the book. We size to stay in the game.
A "cheap" broken stock keeps falling
Falling-knife avoidance. The desk specifically screens out distressed, single-asset stories where a miss tends to spiral — and never buys the bounce.
We're long-only but want to express downside
Paired hedging. Instead of shorting, we pair a long with a correlated counter-position, keeping the book market-aware and within a safe mandate.
The model is confidently wrong
Independent review + human gate. A second desk must agree, disagreements are reconciled, and a human approves — a model can't unilaterally act.
The strategy gets too big to trade
Capacity awareness. The universe is liquidity-screened and the strategy is small-and-nimble by design; we grow only within what the names can absorb.
Weekend / overnight surprises
Weekly flatness. The book is closed each Friday, so we don't carry unmanaged exposure through periods we can't react to.

10

Questions you're probably asking

Isn't this just an AI guessing at stocks?

No. The AI doesn't "predict" — it runs a disciplined process: judge direction from evidence, size the move from five independent methods that must converge, and pass an independent review plus a human gate. The intelligence is in the structure and the growing memory of real precedents, not a black-box hunch. Every decision is traceable to sourced facts.

How do you know this isn't just overfit to the past?

Two safeguards. First, time-stamping: the system can only ever use information that was actually available before each event, so it can't secretly peek at answers. Second, walk-forward testing on periods it never saw, judged by a luck-adjusted measure that penalizes trying many variations. And ultimately, the honest answer is that the forward paper-trading month — brand-new data, in real time — is the test that can't be overfit, and we run it before scaling.

What's your edge versus a big fund with a PhD biotech team?

We're not trying to out-science them on any single drug. Our edge is process and breadth: a systematic, unemotional weekly discipline applied across the whole universe, backed by a memory of hundreds of catalysts that no individual analyst holds in their head — and a fraction of the cost structure. We compete on consistency and reference class, not on being the world's best expert on one molecule.

Why would a playbook built from history work on the future?

Because we use it the way a doctor uses case history — as reference, not prophecy. The desk retrieves past situations that genuinely resemble the new one and reasons from them, applying judgment; it doesn't blindly replay a rule. History rhymes in this space (priced-in reactions, benchmark misses, distressed dynamics recur), and the memory keeps updating with every new week.

What happens in a drawdown?

The strategy is built to make drawdowns survivable: small per-event sizing, weekly flat books (no weekend exposure), falling-knife screens, and a hard stop on any single position dominating the portfolio. We optimize to stay solvent and in the game — the precondition for compounding — rather than to maximize any single week.

What's the capacity — can this scale?

It's deliberately a small, nimble strategy. The universe is screened for liquidity, and we size to what the names can absorb without moving them. This is a high-discipline, capacity-aware strategy, not an unlimited-AUM one — and we'd rather protect returns than chase size.

What's the single biggest risk?

That the edge, once live, proves thinner than the system suggests. We take that seriously — which is exactly why we insist on a real-money-adjacent proving period with full transparency before raising to scale, rather than asking anyone to trust a backtest.
