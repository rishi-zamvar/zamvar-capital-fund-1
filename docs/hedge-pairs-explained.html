Why These Pairs

The plain-English reasoning behind every hedge — no jargon, just what we're betting, what protects us, and how sure we are.

The whole idea in one paragraph

We bet on a company right before a make-or-break announcement (usually whether a drug worked). A bet like that can swing 50–70% overnight. So we don't bet naked — we pair it with a second stock that should go UP if our main bet goes wrong. That way a nasty surprise doesn't wipe us out.

The catch — and it's the whole game: the second stock only protects us if it genuinely benefits from the first one's failure. That means a real competitor fighting for the same patients, where one company's loss is the other's win. If both companies are chasing the same idea the same way, they fall together — and that's a fake hedge, not a real one.

The proof this actually works

We didn't just theorize this — we checked a real event. On July 9, 2026, Ionis announced its heart-drug trial failed. Here's what happened that day to Ionis and two rivals who both sell competing heart drugs:

Ionis (IONS) heart-drug trial failure · July 9, 2026 · one-day moves
Ionisthe company whose drug failed
−24%
BridgeBiosells a competing drug that works a different way
+15%
Alnylamsells a drug that works the same way as the one that failed
−3%

BridgeBio is a real hedge. Its drug works differently, so when the rival approach flopped, doctors were expected to stick with BridgeBio — and it jumped 15% the same day Ionis fell 24%. If we'd been betting against Ionis and holding BridgeBio, the winner would have cushioned the loser.

Alnylam is a fake hedge. Its drug works the same way as the one that failed, so investors got nervous about the whole approach and it fell too. A "competitor" that drops alongside your loser gives you no protection at all.

That single event is the rule the whole system runs on: a real hedge is a competitor with a different approach; a same-approach rival is a trap.

The honest big picture

Then we stress-tested every pair against two years of real prices — and the truth got starker: there is essentially no statistically reliable, similar-sized hedge pair in this corner of biotech. The best one (heart: Ionis/BridgeBio) is mechanism-sound and worked once, but isn't statistically confirmed day-to-day. One pair we expected to work (swelling-disorder: Pharvaris/BioCryst) turned out to move the wrong way — an anti-hedge. Real "one's loss is the other's win" mostly flows to $50-billion giants that barely move.

So the rule is deliberately strict, and it drives how big we bet:

Full size — only in the three real-hedge clusters (heart / swelling-disorder / liver).

Small size, or skip — everything else (cancer, mental-health, muscle, hives): the bet has no reliable protector, so we keep it small enough that a bad surprise is survivable.

A biotech "market umbrella" (an inverse sector fund, LABD) — used sparingly, only to soften a broad sector drop during the event week. It does not protect against one specific drug failing.

The real fix is options. The single biggest upgrade would be adding "insurance contracts" (put options) that pay off when a stock drops — that would hedge the 75% we currently can't. Today's broker connection only allows plain stock buying, so this is the top thing to unlock next.

None of this is a weakness — it's the discipline that stops one bad surprise from becoming a disaster. Betting small when there's no hedge is a feature, not a failure.
